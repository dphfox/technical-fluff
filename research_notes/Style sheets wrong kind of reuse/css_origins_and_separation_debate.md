# The document-centric origins of CSS and the "separation of content and presentation" debate

Scope note: these notes cover (a) what Lie, Bos and the early W3C said style sheets and the cascade were for, (b) CSS Zen Garden and its limits, (c) the 2009–2019 lineage of critiques (OOCSS, SMACSS, BEM, Gallagher, Chedeau, Wathan, Roberts, CSS-in-JS), (d) the "semantic class names" question, (e) the document-vs-application framing, and (f) quotable examples of markup and style co-evolving. Quotes are verbatim from the cited page unless marked "paraphrase". Where a fetch returned only a partial page, that is flagged in Gaps. Numbers in the Lie/Bos book sample were stripped by the PDF's font encoding, so years in those quotes are reconstructed and marked.

## Key question 1: What did Lie, Bos and the early W3C say style sheets and the cascade were FOR?

### Takeaway
The original rationale is explicitly about *documents*: keep HTML a structural language (headings, paragraphs, lists) so content stays reusable across contexts and devices, and let *three independent parties* (author, reader, browser) each contribute style sheets that are "cascaded" because none of them alone can know the final presentation. The late binding of content and style on the reader's machine, and the reader's right to override the author, are the central design goals; "reuse" in the original sense means reuse of *content* under many presentations, not reuse of style rules across UIs.

### Cited Findings

**The 1994 proposal (Håkon Wium Lie, CERN, "Cascading HTML style sheets – a proposal", dated 10 Oct 1994, v0.92)**
- Purpose statement: "Its primary purpose is to establish guiding principles and propose a level of functionality for HTML style sheets." — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)
- The two stated goals are reader-side and author-side: "giving readers a richer visual (or auditory and tactile) environment while they retain control if necessary" and "providing authors and publishers with stylistic influence without resorting to page description languages." — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)
- The problem being solved is HTML's lack of presentation control: "the author of HTML documents has no influence over the presentation", which "has especially been a source of much frustration among professions that are used to be in control of paper-based publishing." — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)
- Existing (browser-resource) style sheets were criticised as "static, they seldom change within the lifetime of a browser process" and "implemented using platform-specific notations, e.g. X11 resources." — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)
- The cascade is defined as an ordered list of sheets from multiple parties: "The proposed scheme supplies the brower with an ordered list (cascade) of style sheets." "Multiple style sheets can be referenced from the same document, and will be appended to the cascade". "the user/browser specifies initial preferences and hands the remaining influence over to the style sheets". — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)
- The original cascade was a *negotiation of percentages*, e.g. `h2.font.size = 20pt 40%`, combined as "a weighted average of the two requests"; the reader-side goal is uniformity across documents: "the user will set preferences to make all documents appear more similar." — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)
- Multi-medium motivation: "HTML -- different from the page description languages -- has the potential of supporting many output media, e.g. paper, speech and braille." Example `speech.em.weight = 40db`. — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)
- Lie credits "Robert Raisch's earlier stylesheet proposal has been influential" and thanks Dave Raggett, Phill Hallam-Baker and Tim Berners-Lee. — [Lie 1994](https://www.w3.org/People/howcome/p/cascade.html)

**W3C's own history ("A brief history of CSS until 2016", Bos/Lie, w3.org)**
- Lie "saw the need for a style sheet language for the Web" after earlier work on personalized newspaper presentation at the MIT Media Lab; the first draft of "Cascading HTML Style Sheets" was published three days before Marc Andreessen announced the first Mozilla beta on 13 Oct 1994. — [W3C history](https://www.w3.org/Style/CSS20/history.html)
- Dave Raggett encouraged release because HTML "should never become a page-description language." — [W3C history](https://www.w3.org/Style/CSS20/history.html)
- The cascade exists because presentation has more than one stakeholder: "the style of a document couldn't be designed by either the author or the reader on their own"; "their wishes had to be combined, or cascaded, in some way." At the Chicago Web conference (Nov 1994) Lie showed "a balance between author and user preferences" illustrated by a slider. — [W3C history](https://www.w3.org/Style/CSS20/history.html)
- At WWW3 Darmstadt (April 1995) the opposing camp argued "the author ultimately had to be in charge of deciding how documents were presented"; Bos and Lie argued "the user ... should be given the last word when conflicts arise." — [W3C history](https://www.w3.org/Style/CSS20/history.html)
- Design-goal lineage: Bert Bos's Argo browser pushed the goal that CSS be "general enough to apply to other markup languages in addition to HTML"; at Developer's Day 1994 critics wanted a full programming language for styling and CSS instead chose "a simple, declarative format." — [W3C history](https://www.w3.org/Style/CSS20/history.html)

**Lie's PhD thesis, "Cascading Style Sheets", University of Oslo, 2005 (published online 2006)**
- Hypothesis: "The hypothesis is that the web calls for different style sheet languages than does traditional electronic publishing." Lie's conclusion: "I believe it has been shown that the hypothesis is true." — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/) (mirror: people.opera.com/howcome/2006/phd/)
- Definition of a style sheet: "a set of rules that associate stylistic properties and values with structural elements in a document". — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Why style sheets were proposed: "Style sheets were proposed as an alternative to the evolution of HTML from a structural language to a presentational language." Presentational tags threatened "device independence, accessibility and content reuse." — [Lie thesis, Introduction](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- The reuse that was intended is *content* reuse: "The benefit of a high abstraction level is that the content can be reused in many contexts." "The drawback of using presentation-oriented document formats is that the content is not easily reusable in other contexts." "the content can be used in many contexts and presented in various ways". "Having separate style sheets eases web-site maintenance and makes documents smaller." — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- "The concept of separating structure from presentation is now firmly established." — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Late binding is the crux of the web case: "The binding no longer takes place in the publishing house but, rather, in the user's computer." — [Lie thesis, Introduction](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- The "Web requirements" chapter lists six requirements derived from web characteristics: later binding → stream-based style sheets ("it is requirement that style sheets can be applied to documents as they are downloaded"); screen-centric publishing → screen-based units ("a pixel unit is required to ensure that (say) a border is exactly one pixel wide"); shared author/user influence → negotiation between conflicting stylistic preferences ("The added flexibility of later binding makes it possible for both users and authors to influence the presentation"); multiple outputs → media-specific style sheets ("Web content is displayed on many types of output devices"); hyperlinked documents → link styling; uncertain delivery → robustness ("If a style sheet fails to appear, the content should still be presentable to the user"). — [Lie thesis, ch. Web requirements](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- The cascade chapter: "CSS, therefore, defines three possible sources for style sheets: authors, readers, and browsers." Users "would like to retain final control of the presentation"; a user "may be particularly interested in reading what the author wants to hide." "Combining style sheets from authors with the browser's default style sheet is a widely used feature of cascading." — [Lie thesis, ch. CSS → Cascading](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- On the CHSS (1994) predecessor: "CHSS supports shared influence between authors and users." "User style sheets are given priority when influence is assigned, and author style sheets come second." "the C in CSS stands for [cascading] which was first described in CHSS." The author "has no way of knowing in advance" device features such as DISPLAY_HEIGHT. — [Lie thesis, ch. Style sheet proposals for the web](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Raisch's 1993 proposal (quoted in the thesis) framed styles as "a set of HINTS or SUGGESTIONS to the renderer which might be used to display particular HTML objects" and said "It is not appropriate to use HTML for this purpose". — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- The thesis's own glossary defines "application" only as "A (somewhat complex) computer program that has a user interface", and the only forward-looking mention is a research question: "Can cascading be used to determine GUI settings of an application?" There is no treatment of web applications anywhere in the thesis. — [Lie thesis, Glossary and Future research](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- The elements CSS was designed around are headings, paragraphs and lists: HTML-0's `P` left presentation open ("The exact representation of this (indentation, leading, etc) is not defined here"); "headings are given a numbered level to indicate their place in the document structure"; `H1`–`H6`, `UL`, `DL`, `DT`, `MENU` are among the original elements; the canonical example is `H1 { color: blue }`. — [Lie thesis, ch. Structured documents](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)

**Lie's own verdict on the cascade's success (thesis, "Problems in CSS" and Conclusions)**
- "Cascading is an ambitious mechanism that has failed to provide users an equal right to influence document presentations." — [Lie thesis, Problems in CSS → Summary](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- "It is impossible to write one user style sheet that cascades well with all author style sheets on the web." ("site abundance" problem) — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- "there is an intrinsic conflict between richness of selectors and cascading." (richer selectors make overriding harder) — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- "CSS offers no such mechanism; each rule in CSS is set independently from the other rules." (no grouping: authors want rules to succeed/fail together) — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- "The class problem is a specific case of the generic markup problem described above." (class attributes produce style sheets that others cannot readily cascade with) — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- What CSS achieved: "CSS is a well-understood style sheet language with several excellent implementations"; CSS "has partially fulfilled its ambition of maintaining HTML as a structured markup language" and helped ensure "that documents can be styled by users." — [Lie thesis, Conclusions](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)

**"Cascading Style Sheets: Designing for the Web" (Lie & Bos; 3rd ed. 2005 sample pages, Addison-Wesley)**
- Robert Cailliau's foreword (first published 1st ed., 1997; "slightly revised" for 2nd ed.): "I'm firmly in the camp of those who think that quality of content comes first and presentation comes later." "Fortunately SGML's philosophy allows us to separate structure from presentation and the Web DTD, HTML, is no exception." "I've always had one concern: is it possible to create a powerful enough style sheet 'language' without ending up with a programming language?" "You have here a book on presentation. But it is presentation of information that should also remain structured so that your content can be effectively used by others while retaining the specific visual aspects you want to give it. Use CSS with care. It is the long-awaited salt on the Web food: a little is necessary, too much is not good cooking." "The efforts of the authors have finally brought us what we sorely needed: the author's ability to shape the content without affecting the structure. This is good news for the Web." — [Lie & Bos 3rd ed. sample PDF](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321193124/samplepages/0321193121.pdf) (text decoded from a font-shifted PDF; digits lost)
- Cailliau also describes the lost reader-control of the first NeXTStep browser: "I decided what font it came out in, how big I wanted the letters, what styles I chose for definition lists and where tabs went. Then we descended into the Dark Ages for several years because the Web exploded into a community that had no idea that such freedom was possible but worried about putting on the remote screen exactly what they thought their information should look like." — [Lie & Bos sample PDF](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321193124/samplepages/0321193121.pdf)
- The 3rd-edition foreword (unsigned in the extracted sample; the 3rd edition's foreword is widely credited to Jeffrey Zeldman, not verified here): "the biggest thing Bert and Håkon did for us – is that CSS separates presentation from underlying structure and semantics. CSS takes the visual instructions out of HTML, where they never belonged anyway, and sticks them into one or more lean, cacheable documents that are powerful enough to present your site one way in a traditional graphic browser, another way in a phone browser, a third way in kiosk mode, and a fourth for your printer." — [Lie & Bos sample PDF](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321193124/samplepages/0321193121.pdf)
- The 3rd-edition preface quotes CSS Zen Garden's "Road to Enlightenment" text and says "we believe that the web is a more enlightened place now that CSS has matured to a stage where it can be used for advanced layouts". The acknowledgements credit "David Raggett and Steven Pemberton were influential in establishing the concept of style sheets for the Web". — [Lie & Bos sample PDF](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321193124/samplepages/0321193121.pdf)

**Bert Bos, "What is a good standard? An essay on W3C's design principles" (6 Mar 2003)**
- Calls "separate structure and style" the most commonly heard Web guideline and says it led to HTML and CSS being separate specifications; attributes the rule to "maintainability, implementability, finding the right experts". The essay opens by asking why CSS does not include "commands to transform a document". It does not discuss applications. — [Bos 2003](https://www.w3.org/People/Bos/DesignGuide/introduction)

### Inferences
- Every one of Lie's six "web requirements" presupposes a *document delivered to a stranger*: the author doesn't know the device, the reader may add preferences, the style sheet may not arrive. None of them describe a UI whose author also controls the runtime. The cascade is a conflict-resolution mechanism between parties who never meet, not a code-organisation mechanism within one team.
- "Reuse" in the founding texts always means the *content* being reusable under new presentations (print, phone, speech, user override). The later industry meaning (reusing style rules across many pieces of markup) is absent; Lie's own "class problem" notes that author-chosen class attributes actively *undermine* the original kind of reuse by making styles non-cascadable for third parties.
- Lie's 2005 verdict that the cascade "has failed to provide users an equal right to influence document presentations" means the strongest original justification for the cascade (user override) was conceded by its inventor to be unrealised, while the mechanism's costs (specificity, source order, global scope) remain.

## Key question 2: What is CSS Zen Garden (Dave Shea, 2003) and what did it demonstrate? What did commentators say about its limits?

### Takeaway
Zen Garden (launched May 2003) proved that a *single fixed HTML document* could be re-skinned into hundreds of radically different designs by swapping only the stylesheet; its own rule was "you may modify the style sheet in any way you wish, but not the HTML." Critics from 2006 onward argued that the demo only works because the markup was pre-padded with hooks for every imaginable design, and that in real projects CSS must be mapped onto a specific DOM structure, so the "separation" is implicit coupling rather than independence.

### Cited Findings
- The site's purpose: "to demonstrate what can be accomplished through CSS-based design" and "CSS allows complete and total control over the style of a hypertext document." Participation rule: "You may modify the style sheet in any way you wish, but not the HTML." Validation is the "only real requirement". — [csszengarden.com](https://csszengarden.com/)
- The "Road to Enlightenment" paragraph (quoted later by Lie & Bos in their 3rd-edition preface): "Littering a dark and dreary road lay the past relics of browser-specific tags, incompatible DOMs, and broken CSS support. Today, we must clear the mind of past practices. Web enlightenment has been achieved thanks to the tireless efforts of folk like the W3C, WaSP and the major browser creators." — [csszengarden.com](https://csszengarden.com/); quoted in [Lie & Bos sample PDF](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321193124/samplepages/0321193121.pdf)
- Shea's 20-year retrospective (About page): in 2003 "the widespread perception of CSS was as a language capable only of boxy and boring design"; the rule was that "a designer was unable to modify the HTML in any way"; the purpose was to argue that "CSS-based design was finally ready for prime time." Catalysts: Chris Casciano's "Daily CSS Fun" restyling exercise, a Wired CSS re-skin contest, and Zeldman's push. The page does not discuss the site's limits; it says the designs "span a formative period of web design and development" and the site "continues on as a reference for web curriculums." — [Zen Garden About](https://www.csszengarden.com/pages/about/)
- Launch May 2003; active development stopped April 2008; reopened 7 May 2013 for the tenth anniversary; 218 listed designs; thousands submitted from over 30 countries; translated into 22 languages; spawned a book. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS_Zen_Garden), [daveshea.com/projects/zen](https://daveshea.com/projects/zen)
- Shea (2013 .net/Creative Bloq interview) said he had avoided updating the site for years because its original purpose — convincing people web standards were worthwhile — had mostly been achieved; the relaunch was prompted partly by a post "about how the site was fading as a teaching tool." — [Creative Bloq](https://creativebloq.com/design/dave-shea-return-css-zen-garden-5135652)
- W3C history: Shea created the Zen Garden "to show fellow graphics artists why CSS should be taken seriously." — [W3C history](https://www.w3.org/Style/CSS20/history.html)
- Early critique (www-html list, 3 July 2006, "Spartanicus", replying to a thread in which David Dorward had described Zen Garden's "redundent divs and classes"): the project erred by "using a single HTML document with only the stylesheet being changed"; "It falsely suggests that CSS as currently implemented" is "capable of fundamentally changing the layout and look of a site"; "To create this illusion it litters the markup with spurious code which makes the code inflexible and difficult to maintain." A reply in the thread countered that the extra divs and classes are not redundant if they are exactly what enables the flexibility. — [Spartanicus, www-html 2006](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-html/2006Jul/0005.html); thread context [Philip Taylor](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-html/2006Jul/0004.html)
- Adam Wathan (2017) names Zen Garden as the canonical model of the "semantic" approach: "The quintessential example of this approach has always been CSS Zen Garden", "a site designed to show that if you 'separate your concerns', you can completely redesign a site just by swapping out the stylesheet." His verdict on that model: "In this model, your HTML is restyleable, but your CSS is not reusable." — [Wathan 2017](https://adamwathan.me/css-utility-classes-and-separation-of-concerns/)
- 2026 revival: Jo Sprague, "The CSS Zen Garden dream, finally shipped" (14 Aug 2026, updated 16 Sep 2026) on rebuilding Firefox.com in plain CSS with 70+ components and 25 page templates, attributes the 20-year delay to CSS's immaturity in 2008 (no variables, inconsistent rendering) and acknowledges the objection that "the Zen Garden dream was about radically different designs from the same markup, and that Firefox.com has only one design." — [Sprague 2026](https://josprague.com/blog/the-css-zen-garden-dream-finally-shipped/)
- Hacker News discussion of that post (Sept 2026): commenter "vehemenz": the Zen Garden model worked because "everyone was using a single markup file"; separating markup from styling "means that the CSS rules need a way to be mapped onto the DOM", and that mapping is implicit; contrasts Tailwind/Tachyons "where nearly all the structure is explicit" and argues hidden structure is technical debt. Commenter "TimTheTinker": HTML and CSS were built for documents, not app development; "Interactivity and web apps came later, and CSS has taken a very long time to catch up." — [HN thread 49713262](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49713262) (via [zeli.app summary](https://zeli.app/story/49713262))
- An academic study of Zen Garden source (listed in search results, not fetched) concludes the CSS "operated within an ecosystem of browsers, screen sizes, and design tools that limited its use." — [search snippet only](https://sindex.sdl.edu.sa/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Demonstrating-and-negotiating-the-adoption-of/9914282808331)

### Inferences
- Zen Garden is the purest instance of the "document of unknown content... or rather, document of *known* content and unknown style" model: the content was frozen and *only* style varied. It therefore demonstrates exactly the direction of freedom that the founding texts cared about (same content, many presentations) and nothing about the opposite direction (same styles, many contents), which is what application UI needs.
- Both the 2006 mailing-list critique and the 2026 HN critique make the same structural point twenty years apart: the demo's "independence" is bought by front-loading the markup with hooks, i.e. by making the HTML know about every style that might ever be applied. That is a form of coupling, not its absence.

- Dave Shea's 2013 mezzoblue post "Ten Years of Zen" returned HTTP 503 and could not be quoted directly; the Creative Bloq interview is the best available secondary source. No statement by Shea himself about Zen Garden's limits as a model for *applications* was found.
- The www-html reply defending the extra divs was summarised by search, not read directly.

## Key question 3: Key critiques of separation-of-concerns as applied to CSS (OOCSS, SMACSS, BEM, Gallagher, Chedeau, Roberts, Wathan, CSS-in-JS)

### Takeaway
From 2007–2009 (BEM at Yandex, Sullivan's OOCSS) through 2011–2014 (SMACSS, Gallagher, Roberts' specificity work, Chedeau's seven problems) to 2017–2019 (Wathan, Dalgleish, Stoiber), practitioners working on large sites and apps converged on the same diagnosis: the cascade is a single global, source-order-dependent namespace with no dependency or scoping mechanism, so at team scale it must be *avoided* (flat specificity, class-only selectors, location-independent components, scoped/generated class names) rather than used. Each methodology is, in effect, a convention for not cascading.

### Cited Findings

**BEM (Yandex, 2005–2009)**
- 2005: Yandex front-end work used static HTML pages with all CSS in one file; HTML fragments were pasted into XSL templates and "all changes were synced two-way, manually." Early 2006 (Yandex.Music): developers faced dozens of class names to invent and "a growing number of unintentional dependencies spreading across the project"; long cascading rules were common, some relying on ID and tag selectors. — [Smashing Magazine, "The Evolution of the BEM Methodology", Feb 2013 (Maxim Shirshin)](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2013/02/the-history-of-the-bem-methodology/)
- Yandex's official history: the projects "revealed the main drawbacks of the current approach": edits to one page could break others, choosing class names was hard, and "a typical CSS back then contained a long cascade of nested selectors." The response was a "common blocks library". — [bem.info history (search summary; direct fetch 404)](https://en.bem.info/method/history), mirror [history.en.md](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/md2xliff@2.0.0/bem-method/method/history/history.en.md)
- Fall 2007 (ClientSide 2007, Moscow): the "independent block" concept — use only class names, not IDs, for CSS; every CSS rule must belong to a block. "Completely independent blocks" went further: "Use class names for everything" and avoid matching CSS to tag names (described as costly, used as a remedy). Early 2008: portal-wide block framework "Lego"; March 2009: Lego 2.0, the rise of "block, element, modifier". — [Smashing Magazine 2013](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2013/02/the-history-of-the-bem-methodology/)

**OOCSS (Nicole Sullivan, first presented at Web Directions North, Denver, 2009)**
- A CSS "object" is "a repeating visual pattern" extractable as an independent snippet of HTML, CSS and possibly JS, reused across a site. Principle 1, "Separate structure and skin": repeating visual features defined as separate skins mixed with objects; "Using classes to name objects and their components, instead of relying on HTML semantics." Principle 2, "Separate container and content": "rarely use location-dependent styles"; "An object should look the same no matter where you put it." — [OOCSS wiki](https://github.com/stubbornella/oocss/wiki)
- Sullivan's framing (Webstock interview): a rounded-corner box "shouldn't specify how a heading inside it is rendered"; the heading is its own object. — [Webstock interview](https://www.webstock.org.nz/the-speaker-interviews-nicole-sullivan/)
- Smashing Magazine's OOCSS intro illustrates the container problem: a rule scoped to `#sidebar h3` cannot be reused for footer headings without duplicating selectors and overriding values. — [Smashing Magazine 2011](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2011/12/an-introduction-to-object-oriented-css-oocss/)
- Contemporary pushback (Ben Nadel, 2009): the second principle "requires developers to modify content using (CSS) class extension rather than context" and the OOCSS framework itself still uses some contextual rules. — [Nadel 2009](https://www.bennadel.com/blog/1633-object-oriented-css-oocss-by-nicole-sullivan.htm)

**SMACSS (Jonathan Snook, written 2011)**
- Motivation: "techniques that can keep CSS more organized and more structured"; "Small sites don't often hit the same pain points as larger sites or working with larger teams". Categories: Base, Layout, Module, State, Theme. — [smacss.com](https://smacss.com/book/)
- "Depth of applicability": "The depth of applicability is the number of generations that are affected by a given rule." "The problem with such a depth is that it enforces a much greater dependency on a particular HTML structure." Example contrasts `#sidebar div h3` with a shallow `.pod`, which is still "a container that still relies on a particular HTML structure." — [SMACSS, Depth of Applicability](https://smacss.com/book/applicability)

**Nicolas Gallagher, "About HTML semantics and front-end architecture" (15 Mar 2012)**
- The HTML5 spec's advice that class values should "describe the nature of the content, rather than values that describe the desired presentation of the content" is, on large sites, "often a hindrance when working on large websites or applications." — [Gallagher 2012](https://nicolasgallagher.com/about-html-semantics-front-end-architecture/)
- "The primary purpose of a class name is to be a hook for CSS and JavaScript." "Class names impart little or no useful semantic information to machines". "Class names should communicate useful information to developers." — [Gallagher 2012](https://nicolasgallagher.com/about-html-semantics-front-end-architecture/)
- "The most reusable components are those with class names that are independent of the content". "The class name news doesn't tell you anything that is not already obvious from the content." Uses Sullivan's media object and a `btn`/`uilist` example where content-coupled selectors prevent "the easy combination of the btn component with the uilist component." Closing: "anyone can rearrange pre-built 'lego blocks'; no one can perform CSS-alchemy." — [Gallagher 2012](https://nicolasgallagher.com/about-html-semantics-front-end-architecture/)

**Harry Roberts (CSS Wizardry)**
- "The Specificity Graph" (29 Oct 2014): specificity is "one of CSS' most troublesome features"; "Specificity is a trait best avoided"; "In general we do want to keep specificity low at all costs"; the goal is to end "erratic and poorly managed specificity-and-source-order" ("the specificity wars visualised"); "No more bolting things on to the end of a stylesheet!"; "A spiky graph is a bad graph." Introduces "my currently unpublished ITCSS architecture". — [Roberts 2014](https://csswizardry.com/2014/10/the-specificity-graph/)
- CSS Guidelines: "CSS is not a pretty language." CSS is "globally operating, very leaky, dependent on location, hard to encapsulate, based on inheritance". "CSS cannot be encapsulated, it is inherently leaky". High specificity "undermines CSS' very nature of the cascade, inheritance, and source order." Location independence: "not to style things based on where they are, but on what they are"; "A component shouldn't have to live in a certain place to look a certain way." On dependencies: "As CSS is something of a declarative language that doesn't really leave much of a paper-trail" it is hard to know "whether some CSS relies on other code elsewhere". — [cssguidelin.es](https://cssguidelin.es/)

**Christopher "vjeux" Chedeau, "React: CSS in JS" (slides dated 8 Nov 2014; NationJS talk posted 15 Nov 2014)**
- Framing: "When I'm saying at scale, it means in a codebase with hundreds of developers that are committing code everyday." The seven problems: (1) Global Namespace — "Bootstrap introduces a whooping 600 global variables"; (2) Dependencies — "there is a very pernicious side effect with CSS" (load-order); (3) Dead Code Elimination — "how do you remove dead code?"; (4) Minification — "minify all the class names"; (5) Sharing Constants — comments used to sync CSS and JS values, "Unfortunately, it doesn't really scale"; (6) Non-deterministic Resolution — "CSS was designed with a single file in mind" and "the last one in the file wins" on specificity ties, which async bundling makes unpredictable; (7) Isolation — developers can "modify the style of the internals via selectors", breaking component maintainers' assumptions. Slide 25: "CSS in JS — The moment you've all been waiting for"; slide 30: "Solved without hacks." The slides never use the word "cascade". — [Chedeau slides](https://speakerdeck.com/vjeux/react-css-in-js); [blog index entry](https://blog.vjeux.com/2014/javascript/react-css-in-js-nationjs.html) (the blog post says the talk "attempts to show that CSS has fundamental flaws")
- Direct reaction: Keith J. Grant, "Against CSS in JS" (29 May 2015), responds to "Christopher Chedeau's slidedeck 'React CSS in JS'" which "outlines seven 'problems' with CSS", calls moving styles into JS "a really bad idea", and defends separation: with HTML "a true separation of concerns between the markup and the corresponding component code is impossible", but "With CSS, this separation is possible and is in fact vital to clean code organization." He argues markup should depend on the stylesheet (a "modular, reusable API"), not the reverse, and that "Every JavaScript library and every HTML templating language works with CSS." — [Grant 2015](https://keithjgrant.com/posts/2015/05/against-css-in-js/)

**Adam Wathan, "CSS Utility Classes and 'Separation of Concerns'" (7 Aug 2017)**
- Phase 1 "'Semantic' CSS": "One of the best practices you'll hear about when you're trying to learn how to CSS good is 'separation of concerns.'" The turning point "came when I read Nicolas Gallagher's" article, after which he was "fully convinced that optimizing for reusable CSS was going to be the right choice." — [Wathan 2017](https://adamwathan.me/css-utility-classes-and-separation-of-concerns/)
- The dependency-direction argument: "Naming your classes based on your content (like `.author-bio`) treats your HTML as a dependency of your CSS." Presentational class names instead treat "your CSS as a dependency of your HTML." "'Separation of concerns' is a straw man"; "This is not the right way to think about HTML and CSS." "For the project you're working on, what would be more valuable: restyleable HTML, or reusable CSS?" "In this model, your HTML is restyleable, but your CSS is not reusable." / "In this model, your CSS is reusable, but your HTML is not restyleable." — [Wathan 2017](https://adamwathan.me/css-utility-classes-and-separation-of-concerns/)
- His five phases: "Semantic" CSS → Decoupling styles from structure → Content-agnostic CSS components → Content-agnostic components + utility classes → Utility-first CSS. Closing: "My experience is that building things utility-first leads to more consistent looking designs than working component-first." — [Wathan 2017](https://adamwathan.me/css-utility-classes-and-separation-of-concerns/)

**CSS-in-JS advocates**
- Mark Dalgleish, "A Unified Styling Language" (SEEK blog, 2017; Code 2017 talk): styles are "not this special case in a different file, in a different language"; the movement is about "trying to avoid that global scope, trying to avoid issues with specificity", scoping becomes "automatic every time you write it"; "The component mindset is really critical to understanding where this movement is coming from"; he discusses "how the relationship between your markup and your styles actually changes in this environment." — [Conffab transcript](https://conffab.com/presentation/a-unified-styling-language/); [Medium post](https://medium.com/seek-blog/a-unified-styling-language-d0c208de2026) (fetch 403)
- Max Stoiber, "Why I Write CSS in JavaScript" (18 Feb 2019): "CSS-in-JS boosts my confidence." "I can add, change and delete CSS without any unexpected consequences." "My changes to the styling of a component will not affect anything else." "If I delete a component, I delete its CSS too." "we automatically sidestep common CSS frustrations such as class name collisions and specificity wars." "Never go on a hunt for CSS affecting your components ever again." — [Stoiber 2019](https://mxstbr.com/thoughts/css-in-js)

### Inferences
- There is a clean lineage: Yandex blocks (2007) → OOCSS (2009) → SMACSS (2011) → Gallagher (2012, cites Sullivan) → Roberts' ITCSS/specificity graph (2014) → Chedeau (2014) → Grant's rebuttal (2015) → Wathan (2017, cites Gallagher and Zen Garden) → Dalgleish/Stoiber (2017–2019). Every step is a response to the same failure mode (uncontrolled global cascade at scale), and each successive step disables more of the cascade: first IDs and tag selectors, then descendant depth, then content-derived names, then cross-file source order, then the shared namespace itself.
- Chedeau's problem 6 ("CSS was designed with a single file in mind") and Lie's "site abundance" problem are two views of the same fact: the cascade's resolution rules assume one author sheet, one user sheet and one UA sheet, not N independently-bundled component sheets.
- Grant's defence is the strongest statement of the original model in modern terms (markup depends on a reusable CSS API); Wathan's reply is that this is one of two legitimate dependency directions, and that the Zen Garden direction only pays off when restyling fixed markup is the thing you actually need.

## Key question 4: The "semantic class names" debate — does content-semantic naming buy reusability or re-stylability in practice?

### Takeaway
The evidence from practitioners is one-directional: content-semantic names (`.news`, `.author-bio`, `#sidebar h3`) make HTML restyleable *in principle* but were repeatedly found to produce duplicated CSS and unintentional cross-page dependencies in practice; nobody in the sources reports a real project that exploited the Zen-Garden-style re-skin of unchanged markup, while several report the opposite cost. The defenders' counter-argument is that CSS-as-API keeps markup portable across templating systems.

### Cited Findings
- HTML5 spec guidance (quoted by Gallagher): class values should "describe the nature of the content, rather than values that describe the desired presentation of the content." — [Gallagher 2012](https://nicolasgallagher.com/about-html-semantics-front-end-architecture/)
- Gallagher's rebuttal: "The class name news doesn't tell you anything that is not already obvious from the content." "The most reusable components are those with class names that are independent of the content". — [Gallagher 2012](https://nicolasgallagher.com/about-html-semantics-front-end-architecture/)
- Wathan's worked example: an article preview that "looked exactly like an author bio": "We can't apply our `.author-bio` classes to our article preview; that wouldn't be semantic." Duplicating the styles "works but of course it's not very DRY." — [Wathan 2017](https://adamwathan.me/css-utility-classes-and-separation-of-concerns/)
- Yandex's experience (2006): with semantic/page-specific naming and nested cascades, "edits to one page could break others" and "choosing class names was hard". — [Smashing Magazine 2013](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2013/02/the-history-of-the-bem-methodology/)
- OOCSS: "Using classes to name objects and their components, instead of relying on HTML semantics". — [OOCSS wiki](https://github.com/stubbornella/oocss/wiki)
- Roberts: class naming should "tell you and your team" about relationships: "Now we can clearly see which classes are and are not related to each other". — [cssguidelin.es](https://cssguidelin.es/)
- Defence of the semantic/API model: Grant (2015): CSS should be "a modular, reusable API" that markup depends on, portable across "Every JavaScript library and every HTML templating language". — [Grant 2015](https://keithjgrant.com/posts/2015/05/against-css-in-js/)
- Lie's own analysis (2005) of why author classes hurt *the original* kind of reuse (third-party/user re-styling): "The class problem is a specific case of the generic markup problem": class-based author styles cannot readily be cascaded with by others, and "It is impossible to write one user style sheet that cascades well with all author style sheets on the web." — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Sprague (2026) reports the one large real-world Zen-Garden-style build found (Firefox.com) and concedes it ships "only one design"; the restyle-without-markup benefit is asserted as a possibility ("anyone could write their own stylesheet"), not an exercised one. — [Sprague 2026](https://josprague.com/blog/the-css-zen-garden-dream-finally-shipped/)

### Inferences
- "Semantic" naming optimises for a consumer who never appears in practice (a third party or user re-styling your unchanged markup) at the expense of the consumer who always does (the same team adding the next screen). Lie's "class problem" shows the semantic-class approach does not even deliver the *original* promise (user sheets), because author classes are opaque to any other cascade participant.
- The debate is really about which artefact is stable: the Zen Garden assumes content is fixed and style varies; application teams find the design system is fixed and content varies.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of restyling-without-markup-change in production codebases was found; evidence is anecdotal (Yandex, Facebook, Wathan's agency work).
- Did not locate a Jeffrey Zeldman / WaSP-era primary text defending semantic class names; the HTML5 spec line (via Gallagher) stands in for that position.

## Key question 5: How did the "document vs application" distinction get articulated?

### Takeaway
The founding CSS texts contain no notion of a web application: Lie's thesis uses "application" only to mean desktop software. The distinction entered the styling debate via the React community (Pete Hunt, JSConf EU 2013: "Templates separate technologies, not concerns"; display logic and markup are cohesive), then Chedeau (2014, "at scale" = hundreds of developers), then the "component mindset" (Dalgleish 2017) and the folk claim that "HTML and CSS were built for documents, not app development."

### Cited Findings
- Lie's thesis glossary: "application: A (somewhat complex) computer program that has a user interface." No occurrence of "web application"; only future-research question: "Can cascading be used to determine GUI settings of an application?" — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Lie's "Web requirements" are all about *documents* delivered to unknown readers/devices ("Web content is displayed on many types of output devices"; "If a style sheet fails to appear, the content should still be presentable"). — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Pete Hunt, "React: Rethinking best practices" (JSConf EU, Sept 2013; notes dated 19 Dec 2013): "Templates encourage a poor separation of concerns"; "Templates separate technologies, not concerns"; principle "reduce coupling, increase cohesion"; "build components, not templates"; "view partials" are "evil" (dependency and maintainability hell). The view-model binds controller to template so changing the view usually means updating the controller too. — [Strumpflohner notes 2013](https://juri.dev/blog/2013/12/react-rethingking-best-practices); slides [SlideShare](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/react-preso-v2/26589373) (page failed to load)
- A secondhand claim (Meteor forum commenter) that Hunt cited Separation of Concerns as the React team's main reason for putting styling, logic and presentation in one file could not be verified against the talk. — [search summary](https://forums.meteor.com/t/video-why-doesnt-react-have-templates/17534)
- Critique of the reframing: Andrew Ray argues "'Separation of technologies' isn't a computer science principle" and any template library compiles to JavaScript anyway. — [Ray](https://andrewray.me/blog/reacts-jsx-doesnt-violate-separation-of-concerns)
- Chedeau's scale definition: "a codebase with hundreds of developers that are committing code everyday"; "CSS was designed with a single file in mind". — [Chedeau slides](https://speakerdeck.com/vjeux/react-css-in-js)
- Dalgleish: "The component mindset is really critical to understanding where this movement is coming from." — [Conffab transcript](https://conffab.com/presentation/a-unified-styling-language/)
- Folk articulation (HN, 2026, "TimTheTinker"): HTML and CSS were built for documents, not app development; "Interactivity and web apps came later, and CSS has taken a very long time to catch up." — [HN 49713262](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49713262)
- Cailliau's 1997 foreword already frames the concern as *information*: "it is presentation of information that should also remain structured so that your content can be effectively used by others". — [Lie & Bos sample PDF](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321193124/samplepages/0321193121.pdf)

### Inferences
- The founders' model has three *parties* (author/reader/browser) and one *artefact* (the document); the application model has one party (the product team) and many artefacts (components). The cascade solves inter-party conflict; application teams have no inter-party conflict and so experience the cascade purely as cost.
- Hunt's "technologies, not concerns" line is the hinge: it relocates the unit of concern from the *language* (HTML/CSS/JS) to the *component*, after which "separation of content and presentation" stops being a concern boundary at all.

### Gaps
- The specific Chedeau remark "the DOM is a document model" asked about in the brief was not found in any fetched source; it may be from a different talk (e.g. React Europe) or misattributed. Treat as unverified.
- No Dan Abramov primary text on documents vs apps was fetched; search did not surface one on this topic.
- The WHATWG "Web Applications 1.0" (2004) history, which is the standards-side origin of the "web apps" framing, was not researched.

## Key question 6: Quotable examples of styles and markup needing to co-evolve (the decoupling is illusory)

### Takeaway
The best quotable material comes from Wathan (changing a layout now means editing the HTML, and composing existing components is better than inventing new semantic classes), SMACSS (deep selectors enforce "a much greater dependency on a particular HTML structure"), the 2006 Zen Garden critique (markup "littered" with hooks to create the "illusion" of independence), and the 2026 HN remark that CSS "rules need a way to be mapped onto the DOM" and that this mapping is implicit.

### Cited Findings
- Wathan on layout change forcing markup edits: "Before, we could just open up our stylesheet and choose new styles for either of the two components." "Now we'd need to edit the HTML!" "If we want to change our form from a stacked-form to a horizontal-form, we do it in the markup, not the CSS." And on composition: "But if we already have a card class, why don't we compose this new UI using our existing card and stacked form?" — [Wathan 2017](https://adamwathan.me/css-utility-classes-and-separation-of-concerns/)
- Wathan's dependency framing (both directions are coupling): "Naming your classes based on your content (like `.author-bio`) treats your HTML as a dependency of your CSS." — [Wathan 2017](https://adamwathan.me/css-utility-classes-and-separation-of-concerns/)
- SMACSS: "The problem with such a depth is that it enforces a much greater dependency on a particular HTML structure." Even the shallow `.pod` is "a container that still relies on a particular HTML structure." — [SMACSS](https://smacss.com/book/applicability)
- Zen Garden critique (2006): "To create this illusion it litters the markup with spurious code which makes the code inflexible and difficult to maintain." — [Spartanicus 2006](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-html/2006Jul/0005.html)
- HN (2026): separating markup from styling "means that the CSS rules need a way to be mapped onto the DOM", the mapping is implicit, and hidden structure is technical debt; utility frameworks make "nearly all the structure explicit". — [HN 49713262](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49713262)
- Chedeau's isolation problem: consumers can "modify the style of the internals via selectors", so a component's internal markup becomes a public contract. — [Chedeau slides](https://speakerdeck.com/vjeux/react-css-in-js)
- Roberts: "A component shouldn't have to live in a certain place to look a certain way." and CSS is "dependent on location, hard to encapsulate". — [cssguidelin.es](https://cssguidelin.es/)
- Yandex (2005): HTML and CSS were separate files but "all changes were synced two-way, manually." — [Smashing Magazine 2013](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2013/02/the-history-of-the-bem-methodology/)
- Hunt: the view-model couples template to controller so that changing the view usually means updating the controller too; markup and display logic "both show the UI" (slide summary). — [Strumpflohner notes](https://juri.dev/blog/2013/12/react-rethingking-best-practices); [search summary of slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/react-preso-v2/26589373)
- Lie himself (1994) built the original model on the author *not knowing* the rendering context: DISPLAY_HEIGHT "is a feature of the output device, which the author has no way of knowing in advance." — [Lie thesis on CHSS](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)

### Inferences
- Lineage map for the blog author:
  1. Raisch 1993 → Lie 10 Oct 1994 proposal → Chicago Nov 1994 slider demo → Darmstadt April 1995 author-vs-user dispute → CSS1 (1996) → Cailliau foreword 1997 → Lie thesis 2005 (admits cascade "failed" to give users equal influence).
  2. Zen Garden May 2003 (Shea) → quoted approvingly in Lie & Bos 3rd ed. 2005 → www-html critique July 2006 → Wathan 2017 names it the "quintessential" semantic model → Sprague/Firefox.com Aug–Sept 2026 and HN pushback.
  3. Yandex blocks 2007 → OOCSS 2009 → SMACSS 2011 → Gallagher 2012 (builds on Sullivan) → Roberts 2014 → Chedeau Nov 2014 → Grant May 2015 (rebuttal) → Wathan Aug 2017 (builds on Gallagher, answers Grant's dependency argument) → Dalgleish 2017 / Stoiber 2019.
  4. Hunt Sept 2013 ("technologies, not concerns") → Ray rebuttal → Dalgleish "component mindset".
- The strongest single contrast for the argument: Lie's requirement that "the author has no way of knowing in advance" the rendering context, versus Chedeau's component maintainer whose problem is that *other people in the same codebase* know too much about the internals. The cascade was built for mutual ignorance; application UI is a regime of mutual knowledge.
