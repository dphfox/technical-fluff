# The steelman case FOR the cascade, inheritance and global style sheets (and against component-sealed styling)

Scope note for the writer: these notes represent the *opposing* position to the blog thesis ("style sheets are the wrong kind of reuse in UI"). Every item is sourced; where I could not verify an attributed quote I say so. A recurring methodological warning: several widely-circulated "Miriam Suzanne said X" lines are actually from other authors (Mads Stoumann, Stephanie Eckles, Bramus, Una Kravets) writing *about* her specs. I flag these.

Overall framing that emerged from the sources: the cascade's defenders do not primarily argue "global is tidier than local." They argue (1) CSS was designed as a *negotiation* among three parties who do not know each other (browser, user, author), and encapsulation deletes two of them; (2) inheritance + element selectors are how you get consistency without repeating yourself ("do most of our styling with the least of our code"); (3) the industry's own encapsulation experiment (Shadow DOM) has had to reintroduce escape hatches (custom properties, `::part`, open-stylable roots, `::theme()`) because sealed components cannot be themed or made accessible from outside; and (4) new cascade primitives (`@layer`, `@scope`) were deliberately designed as *weak* scoping that lets global styles "flow through," i.e. the spec authors looked at hard encapsulation and chose not to.

---

## KQ1. Defences of the cascade as a feature (Suzanne, Pickering, Keith, Simmons/Andrew, Bell, Coyier, PPK, Lie)

### Takeaway
The strongest defenders frame the cascade as the mechanism that lets *unknown* parties (browser defaults, user preferences, author brand, third-party code) co-author a presentation without coordinating, and inheritance/element selectors as the mechanism that lets you state a design rule once ("the measure never exceeds 60ch") and have it apply everywhere with only exceptions enumerated. The claim is not that global beats local, but that a system which only has local has thrown away the thing CSS was invented to do.

### Cited Findings

**Håkon Wium Lie (inventor of CSS), PhD thesis, 2005/2006** — the original design rationale.
- "The concept of cascading allowed both authors and users to influence the presentation of a document." … "Negotiating between the needs and wishes of readers and authors was one of the main ambitions of CSS." — [Lie, *Cascading Style Sheets*, PhD thesis, ch. 1](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- On the 1994 CHSS proposal: "The proposed scheme supplies the browser with an ordered list (cascade) of style sheets." … "The user supplies the initial sheet which may request total control of the presentation" … "User style sheets are given priority when influence is assigned, and author style sheets come second." — [Lie, thesis (CHSS section)](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Explicit anti-repetition rationale for cascading: a browser base sheet holds conventions so "users and authors do not need to repeat all desired stylistic rules … Thus, cascading reduces the length of style sheets since accepted conventions do not have to be repeated." — [Lie, thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- On a competing 1990s proposal (JEP): "Browsers are encouraged to provide users with the ability to configure the default style sheet." … "By allowing the user a wider range of weights, the user has the final word." — [Lie, thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)

**Jeremy Keith, "Influence" (adactio.com, 10 Aug 2020; cross-posted to DEV 26 Aug 2020)** — the "cascade = conversation between designer and user" argument.
- Recounts Lie's Oct 1994 "influence" proposal where an author could claim e.g. 100% or 40% influence and "a weighted average of the two requests is calculated"; quotes Lie: the user's sheet "hands most of the influence over to the style sheets referenced in the incoming document" and "the user will set preferences to make all documents appear more similar." — [Keith, "Influence"](https://dev.to/adactio/influence-2cda)
- Keith's conclusion: user style sheets have died out and "Web design today is dictatorial"; every line of CSS is "a suggestion to a web browser—not a demand"; he wishes web design were "more of a conversation between designer and end user." — [Keith, "Influence"](https://dev.to/adactio/influence-2cda)
- Keith recommends Hidde de Vries's talk "On the origin of cascades (by means of natural selectors)" (DotCSS, Dec 2019; CSS Café, Jul 2020) which traces the cascade's 25-year evolution using a Darwin analogy. — [Keith](https://dev.to/adactio/influence-2cda); [slides on Notist](https://noti.st/hdv/2gDDUr/slides); [SitePoint listing](https://www.sitepoint.com/premium/tech-talks/dotcss-2019/on-the-origin-of-cascades/)

**Jeremy Keith, "Declarative design" (adactio.com, 14 Apr 2022)** — the philosophical split.
- Declarative approaches (Utopia, Every Layout, intrinsic web design) "all focus on creating the right *inputs* rather than trying to control every possible *output*"; Tailwind-style imperative tooling "is *only* about the specific outputs." — [Keith, "Declarative design"](https://adactio.com/journal/18982)
- He names the two philosophies explicitly: "CSS is broken and I want my tools to work around the way CSS has been designed" vs "CSS is awesome and I want my tools to amplify the way that CSS had been designed," and says "It all depends on whether the philosophy behind the tool matches your own philosophy." He places himself in the second camp, in the lineage of "A Dao of Web Design" and "Responsive Web Design." Quotes Andy Bell's line "Be the browser's mentor, not its micromanager." — [Keith](https://adactio.com/journal/18982)

**Jeremy Keith, *Resilient Web Design* (2016), ch. 2 "Materials"** — error-tolerance as a feature of the declarative layer.
- Lie "proposed a new format to describe the presentation of HTML documents"; on unknown selectors a browser "simply skips over whatever is between that selector's curly braces"; on unknown declarations "it just ignores that particular declaration"; "The browser does not throw an error." Authors "can rest assured that old browsers will react to new features with complete indifference," and "this loose error-handling has allowed CSS to grow over time." — [Resilient Web Design, ch. 2](https://resilientwebdesign.com/chapter2/)
- Note: this chapter does **not** discuss the cascade mechanism itself; "Cascading" appears only in the name. Don't attribute cascade-balancing claims to this book.

**Heydon Pickering, "CSS: A New Kind Of JavaScript" (12 Jul 2018, originally Medium)** — satire that inverts CSS-in-JS marketing.
- Framing line (quoted by Jeremy Keith, 13 Jul 2018): "We waste far too much time writing and maintaining styles with JavaScript, and I think it's time for a change." — [Keith's link post](https://adactio.com/links/14129)
- Pitches selectors as declarative mass-application: "The above example affects all DOM nodes with the `disabled` attribute automatically"; when the attribute is removed "the default styles are automatically reinstated." — [Pickering](https://heydonworks.com/article/css:-a-new-kind-of-javascript/)
- "The cascading part is perhaps its best feature." CSS is "parsed completely separately to standard JavaScript files," so you can "brand your applications without having to touch your business logic!" Closing jab: "are you willing to embrace the change, or are you married to an inferior methodology?" — [Pickering](https://heydonworks.com/article/css:-a-new-kind-of-javascript/)
- Secondary coverage: CSS-Tricks called it "wacky and satirical" and noted CSS-in-JS debate was "hot and quite active"; Brad Frost praised how it "flips this all on its head." — [CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/css-a-new-kind-of-javascript/); [Brad Frost](https://bradfrost.com/blog/link/css-a-new-kind-of-javascript/)

**Heydon Pickering & Andy Bell, *Every Layout* (every-layout.dev)** — the "axioms" / exception-based styling argument. This is the clearest statement of "inheritance reduces repetition."
- "Unless your design is founded on axioms, your output will be inconsistent and malformed." Example axiom: measure never exceeds 60ch, applied "without qualifications or exceptions." Applying such rules by hand per element is "laborious, prone to error (missing elements out), and will lead to bloated markup." — [Every Layout, "Axioms"](https://every-layout.dev/rudiments/axioms/)
- Exception-based styling: apply the rule broadly, then "we only have to remember which elements should not be subject to the rule"; this lets you "do most of our styling with the least of our code." Citing ITCSS: "specificity (how specific selectors are) is inversely proportional to reach (how many elements they should affect)." Defining the measure as a custom property on `:root` means "any change to the value will be propagated throughout the design." — [Every Layout, "Axioms"](https://every-layout.dev/rudiments/axioms/)
- Element selectors are "still 'global' because they can *reach* those elements wherever they are situated," and their use is "the hallmark of a comprehensive design system." Rules on `:root`/`<body>` are "inherited globally (with just a few exceptions)." Utilities "are for final adjustments"; the authors "highly recommend" not "including utility classes until you need them." Inline styles: "A maintenance nightmare." On Shadow DOM: "you can no longer leverage global styling" (listed as a drawback); their stated goal is to "simultaneously leverage global styling, but apply local, instance-specific styles in a controlled fashion." — [Every Layout, "Global and local styling"](https://every-layout.dev/rudiments/global-and-local-styling/)

**Andy Bell, CUBE CSS (cube.fyi)**
- "a CSS methodology that's orientated towards simplicity, pragmatism and consistency"; "an extension of CSS rather than a reinvention of CSS"; "the cascade and inheritance are *embraced*, not avoided," contrasted with BEM which "run[s] against the grain of CSS"; "by the time you get down to blocks in CUBE, they become much more insignificant." — [CUBE CSS](https://cube.fyi/)

**Andy Bell, "CSS doesn't suck" (9 Jan 2019)**
- CSS lets you use "global styles and the cascade, so you don't end up endlessly repeating yourself"; CSS can "gracefully continue on failure" and "write global and scoped code." The critique "you can't build components that scale efficiently" is "utter poppycock" (cites BEM/SMACSS at scale with many contributors). "It's a bit of a trend—particularly in the JavaScript community—to crap on CSS wherever possible"; the not-component-friendly view is "so often used as an excuse to fall into a JavaScript-based approach for styling components"; "JavaScript is not designed to style elements on a web page, but CSS is." — [Bell, "CSS doesn't suck"](https://archive.hankchizljaw.com/wrote/css-doesnt-suck/) (original URL andy-bell.design redirects here); Baldur Bjarnason's excerpt: "For some reason, amazing features of CSS like this are often seen as a negative in the JavaScript community" — [Bjarnason note](https://notes.baldurbjarnason.com/2019/01/09/css-doesnt-suck.html)

**Andy Bell, "Be the browser's mentor, not its micromanager" (buildexcellentwebsit.es, 2022)**
- "Give the browser some solid rules and hints"; "We are all guilty of micromanaging the browser in some aspects"; prefers CSS that is "flexible, portable" over "rigid, inflexible and overly-specific"; write fluid type/space rules once so sizes adapt to any viewport. — [buildexcellentwebsit.es](https://buildexcellentwebsit.es/)

**Peter-Paul Koch (quirksmode), "Why is not using the CSS cascade a problem?" (17 Apr 2018)** — the explicit "global scope is the strength" statement.
- A shared `button.primary` rule styles every matching button on the page: "This is the strength of the cascade." After thirty minutes with a designer "both of us could drop buttons into the code from that time on, and their styles would just work." "part of the strength of CSS is exactly its cascade-induced global scope." — [PPK](https://www.quirksmode.org/blog/archives/2018/04/css_global_scop.html)
- Crucially, PPK concedes the component case: when a component's button must follow the component's style, general rules get in the way and the component needs local scope. Endorses Charlie Owen's hybrid: set "default block level margins, your typography, etc high up" and let components scope anything extra — "the correct solution to the problem." He also doesn't think JS devs misunderstand the cascade; they understand it and choose not to use it, and he asks whether that is really a problem. — [PPK](https://www.quirksmode.org/blog/archives/2018/04/css_global_scop.html)

**Chris Coyier, "The Great Divide" (CSS-Tricks, Jan 2019)** — cultural, not technical.
- "there is a divide happening in front-end development" between JS-centred developers and those focused on HTML/CSS/design/accessibility. Quotes Mandy Michael: "we need to stop devaluing CSS and HTML." Heydon Pickering on document structure rarely being taught alongside control structure: "It's not their competency, but we still make it their job." Bastian Allgeier: big-team tooling choices can be "highly toxic for smaller teams." Coyier: "Nobody is allowed to do a bad job." — [Coyier](https://css-tricks.com/the-great-divide/)

**Miriam Suzanne (CSSWG; co-editor of Cascade Layers, Container Queries, Scope)**
- Talk "Hints and Suggestions: The Design of Web Design" (beyond tellerrand Berlin, Nov 2024; video posted 2 Dec 2024; reprised as "The Design of Web Design" at Smashing Meets CSS, 1 Apr 2025): the web is "built around a radical political vision for resilience, adaptability, and user control"; the cascade "takes on an almost absurd task – styling unknown content, with unknown collaborators, on an infinite and unknowable canvas, across browsers, languages, writing modes, and device interfaces." Promises the audience will "see the 'CSS is Awesome' meme in a new light." — [beyond tellerrand](https://beyondtellerrand.com/blog/btconf-berlin-2024-video-miriam-suzanne); [Smashing Meets](https://smashingconf.com/meets-modern-css/speakers/miriam-suzanne/)
- PodRocket episode (22 May 2025) description: covers "CSS origins, the cascade, accessibility, and how the balance between user control and brand expression defines the spirit of the web." (Show's words, not hers.) — [PodRocket](https://podrocket.logrocket.com/design-of-web-design-miriam-suzanne)
- Her OddBird author page tagline is "Cascading Style Systems." — [oddbird.net/authors/miriam](https://oddbird.net/authors/miriam)
- Her Cascade Layers explainer frames origins/`!important` as deliberate stakeholder balancing: "Cascade Origins help to balance styling concerns across stakeholders" … origins and `!important` are "designed to solve that same problem on a larger scale" … "It's a pretty clever solution, with `!important` providing much-needed counter-balance." — [Suzanne, Cascade Layers explainer](https://css.oddbird.net/layers/explainer/)
- Her Scope explainer states the design intent directly: "Shadow-DOM is not designed for the common 'design system' approach," where global styles are expected to flow through; "We have intentionally gone in the other direction, making scope proximity less powerful than specificity in the cascade"; the goal is to let global styles "flow through, interact with, and even override scoped styles" and to "prevent scoped styles from leaking out, without getting in the way of global patterns." — [Suzanne, Scope explainer](https://css.oddbird.net/scope/explainer/)
- **Misattribution warnings:** (a) CSS-Tricks "Don't Fight the Cascade, Control It!" (10 Jan 2022) is by **Mads Stoumann**, not Suzanne; its closing line is "the CSS cascade is no longer the enemy it's often made to be," and it quotes Bramus: "The true power of Cascade Layers comes from its unique position in the Cascade." — [Stoumann](https://css-tricks.com/dont-fight-the-cascade-control-it/). (b) Smashing's "Getting Started With CSS Cascade Layers" (24 Jan 2022) is by **Stephanie Eckles**; the only Suzanne quote in it is from her explainer ("Cascade Layers would allow authors to define their own layering scheme and avoid specificity or source-order…"); Eckles notes layers "are not meant to solve scoping or encapsulation." — [Eckles](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/01/introduction-css-cascade-layers/)

**Jen Simmons, Intrinsic Web Design (2018)**
- Big Web Show transcript (2 May 2018): intrinsic web design is "Responsive Web Design bonus edition, with extra super powers"; "You don't have to use media queries to make these things happen"; "This is about having solid technology supporting the web and letting the technology tell us what the medium is"; "Grid gives us ways to actually define that flexibility." — [Zeldman transcript](https://zeldman.com/2018/05/02/transcript-intrinsic-web-design-with-jen-simmons-the-big-web-show/)
- Keith's summary (secondary): intrinsic design = "responsive web design +": fluid *and* fixed images, fluid rows as well as columns, media queries not necessarily needed; the vocabulary ("sizing," "intrinsic") came out of a Rachel Andrew talk. — [Keith on Medium](https://adactio.medium.com/676d2799bd02)
- Caveat: Simmons' material is about *layout algorithms*, not the cascade per se; it supports the "let the browser compute outputs from declared inputs" philosophy rather than a direct defence of global style sheets.

**Den Odell, "We Keep Reinventing CSS, but Styling Was Never the Problem" (denodell.com; summarised by Conffab 17 Sep 2025)**
- "every option comes with trade-offs. None of them quite fit." … "We keep reinventing CSS as if it's the root cause … It isn't." Lists BEM, OOCSS, CSS-in-JS, utility-first as 20 years of attempts to tame globally-operating inheritance/cascade/specificity. — [Conffab summary](https://conffab.com/elsewhere/we-keep-reinventing-css-but-styling-was-never-the-problem/) (I did not fetch the original; the summary doesn't say what Odell thinks the real problem is.)

### Inferences
- The pro-cascade argument is at its strongest when phrased as "three-party negotiation" (Lie, Keith, Suzanne) rather than "global is tidier." A component system with sealed styles has, by construction, no slot for the *user* party and no slot for the *browser default* party; the blog thesis needs to say what replaces that negotiation.
- "Inheritance reduces repetition" is argued concretely only by Lie (cascading avoids restating conventions) and Every Layout (axioms + exceptions, specificity ∝ 1/reach). Everyone else asserts it. The Mazinanian duplication data in KQ6 is the only quantitative evidence on either side, and it cuts *against* real-world CSS (66% median duplication), so the steelman is "CSS as designed" not "CSS as practised."
- PPK and Charlie Owen's hybrid (typography/margins/defaults high up, components scope the rest) is the mainstream pro-cascade position; nobody serious argues components should have *no* local styles. The blog's opponent is not "all styles global" but "the base layer should be a cascade, not copied into each component."

### Gaps
- Could not locate any piece titled "Why CSS is not broken" by Heydon Pickering, nor Jeremy Keith's "the web is a document" as a specific essay; Keith's "Resilient Web Design" does not discuss the cascade mechanism. Treat those attributions in the brief as unverified.
- No primary text by Rachel Andrew or Eric Meyer defending the cascade was retrieved (Meyer's *CSS: The Definitive Guide* covers it, and a search surfaced a Meyer ebook "Selectors, Specificity, and the Cascade" on "how reader and browser preferences affect your page presentation," but I have no quotes). Nicole Sullivan's 2011 "Scope donuts" (stubbornella.org) surfaced in search as early precedent for donut scoping but was not fetched.
- No transcript of Suzanne's "Design of Web Design" talk; only abstracts. The YouTube video linked from PodRocket would be the primary source for her actual wording on "hints and suggestions."

## KQ2. Use cases where a global cascade is essential or far better — split by "unknown content" vs "unknown style author"

### Takeaway
The cases that most embarrass sealed-component styling are the "unknown style author" ones: user style sheets, forced-colors/high-contrast, reader modes, WCAG text-spacing overrides, and whole theming ecosystems (Stylus, Obsidian, BetterDiscord) that exist *because* page styles are reachable. "Unknown content" cases (CMS/markdown, third-party embeds) are real but weaker, because a component system can wrap unknown content in a "prose" component. VS Code is the notable counterexample: its theming is token-JSON, not CSS, which supports the blog's thesis that a token layer can replace a style sheet for *theming*.

### Cited Findings

**The cascade's user tier is a standardised accessibility mechanism (unknown style author).**
- MDN cascade order (low→high): user-agent normal, user normal, author normal, keyframes, author `!important`, user `!important`, user-agent `!important`, transitions. So user `!important` beats author `!important`. "In most browsers, the user (or reader) of the website can choose to override styles using a custom **user stylesheet** designed to tailor the experience to the user's wishes." — [MDN, Introduction to the CSS cascade](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/CSS_cascade/Cascade)
- WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.12 Text Spacing requires no loss of content/functionality when users set line height ≥1.5× font size, paragraph spacing ≥2×, letter spacing ≥0.12×, word spacing ≥0.16×. Users "may choose a user stylesheet, bookmarklet, extension, or application"; "this SC does not require that content implement its own mechanisms to allow users to do this"; the content fails only if it is "actively and purposely preventing the properties from being added." — [W3C Understanding SC 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/text-spacing.html)
  - Inference: the SC presumes author CSS is *overridable from outside* by a global mechanism. A component whose text spacing is set via inline style or sealed shadow styles is exactly the thing that "actively prevents" the override.

**Forced-colors / Windows High Contrast (unknown style author; the *browser* rewrites colours).**
- `forced-colors` "is used to detect if the user agent has enabled a forced colors mode where it enforces a user-chosen limited color palette on the page." Intended usage: "small tweaks to improve usability or legibility when the default application of forced colors does not work well for a given portion of a page." Baseline across browsers since Sept 2022. — [MDN forced-colors](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/forced-colors)
- Practitioner guidance: "outline is preserved in forced colours; box-shadow is stripped"; avoid "slapping `forced-color-adjust: none` across large regions, defeating the mode for everything inside." — [Ben Myers encyclopedia](https://benmyers.dev/encyclopedia/forced-colors-mode/); [Sparkbox](https://sparkbox.com/foundry/supporting_high_contrast_mode)
- Shadow DOM components must each carry their own forced-colors handling: Microsoft Fluent UI Web Components targets `:host` inside `@media (forced-colors: active)` within the component's own shadow stylesheet and conditionally attaches those sheets when the query matches. — [Fluent UI high-contrast docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/pt-br/fluent-ui/web-components/design-system/high-contrast)
  - Inference: with a global sheet, one `@media (forced-colors: active)` block can fix borders/outlines site-wide; with sealed components every component re-implements it. This is the "every component sets its own X" anti-pattern in accessibility form.

**Reader modes (unknown style author; author CSS removed entirely).**
- Sara Soueidan (10 May 2021): reader modes and forced-color modes are "two common environments where content is typically stripped of our CSS"; tools "strip our content of our CSS and apply their own styles to it"; text in `::before` disappears; advice: "provide real content in HTML, and leave CSS pseudo-elements for decorational content." — [Soueidan](https://sarasoueidan.com/blog/tips-for-reader-modes/)
  - Note: this is arguably evidence *for* the blog's thesis too (reader mode proves presentation is disposable) — but it only works because the author's styles are a separable, removable layer.

**GOV.UK Frontend (standards-driven site, explicit support for colour overrides).**
- GOV.UK Frontend "also supports: your users overriding colours in Windows, Firefox and Chrome," and lists "recommended assistive technologies" and graded browser support. — [GOV.UK Frontend accessibility](https://frontend.design-system.service.gov.uk/accessibility/)
- PR #2264 (focus states in forced colours): "In forced colors mode, transparent borders become solid (matching the text / foreground color) and so we get an additional outline around the focused control"; IE11 handled via `-ms-high-contrast`; radios/checkboxes "not very visible in high contrast mode or when colours are overridden in Firefox." — [alphagov/govuk-frontend PR 2264](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-frontend/pull/2264)
  - Caveat: GOV.UK Frontend is itself a component library (Sass + Nunjucks macros); its global Sass settings/tokens are what let these fixes be made once. It is evidence for "central style layer," not for "no components."

**User-theming ecosystems built on reachable CSS (unknown style author).**
- Stylus: "a browser extension to restyle the websites via CSS themes"; styles from userstyles.world, USO archive, Greasy Fork; UserCSS from any URL. — [openstyles/stylus](https://github.com/openstyles/stylus)
- Obsidian themes: a folder with `manifest.json` + `theme.css`; "Obsidian uses CSS variables to style the user interface"; "Obsidian exposes more than 400 different CSS variables"; variables scoped under `.theme-dark`/`.theme-light`. — [Obsidian docs](https://docs.obsidian.md/Themes/App+themes/Build+a+theme)
  - Note the hybrid: Obsidian theming is *token-shaped* (400+ custom properties) but delivered as a *global CSS file* that can also override any selector. This is exactly the "token escape hatch inside a cascade" pattern.
- Jim Nielsen (2 May 2021): global CSS is an "unofficial [styling API] without any kind of semver contract" — open devtools, find the element, write a selector; because it works the same on every site "custom user style sheets are possible." Shadow DOM means "people can't peer into your component and write selectors that target the things they want to style," and components "can't guarantee any consistency in style APIs from one component to the next." — [Nielsen](https://blog.jim-nielsen.com/2021/shadow-dom-and-the-styling-api/)

**The counterexample: VS Code themes are token JSON, not CSS.**
- VS Code colour themes are JSON with a `colors` object mapping named workbench colour IDs to hex values plus `tokenColors` for syntax; users override via `workbench.colorCustomizations`; "The page doesn't mention CSS." — [VS Code Color Theme guide](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/extension-guides/color-theme)
  - Inference: VS Code shows a mature, user-themable UI with no cascade exposed. This directly supports the blog's thesis *for the theming use case* — but note VS Code themes cannot restyle layout, spacing or typography of arbitrary UI, only named colour slots. The cascade-defender's reply is that token-only theming is a strict subset of what Stylus/Obsidian/userstyles allow.

**Unknown content: CMS / markdown / third-party.**
- Suzanne's Scope explainer motivates donut scope with nested components and "slots" and says Shadow DOM "is not designed for the common 'design system' approach" where global styles flow through. — [Scope explainer](https://css.oddbird.net/scope/explainer/)
- Fagnani's open-stylable issue lists "Legacy pages that expect global styles to apply deeply throughout the DOM tree" and "Modern tooling that doesn't work with scoped styles" (Tailwind) as the drivers. — [WICG #909](https://github.com/WICG/webcomponents/issues/909)
- Matuzo: encapsulation helps most "when you work on a design system and don't know who's using your components" — i.e. the *unknown consumer* case is the one Shadow DOM is good for, which is the mirror image of the unknown-author case. — [Matuzo, 23 Aug 2023](https://matuzo.at/blog/2023/pros-and-cons-of-shadow-dom)

### Inferences
- Classification for the writer:
  - **Unknown style author / user override** (hardest for the blog thesis): user style sheets (MDN, WCAG 1.4.12), forced-colors (MDN, Fluent, GOV.UK), reader modes (Soueidan), Stylus/userstyles, Obsidian and BetterDiscord themes (CSS-based). These require that *someone who never saw the source* can override presentation after the fact. A token layer helps only if the overrider's intent maps onto a pre-declared token.
  - **Unknown content** (addressable by components): markdown/CMS output, third-party embeds, legacy pages. Here the pro-cascade argument is weaker; a "prose"/"rich-text" wrapper component with element selectors inside it (i.e. `@scope`) handles it. Suzanne's own spec treats this as a *scope* problem, not an *isolation* problem.
  - **Token-shaped theming** (VS Code, Obsidian's 400 variables, Shadow DOM custom properties): supports the blog thesis that theming can be a token contract, but every CSS-based example also keeps selector-level override as a fallback.
- The blog author can concede the user-override class and still hold the thesis if the UI platform in question (e.g. Roblox) has *no* user-override tier at all; the honest framing is "the cascade's user tier is valuable on the web and absent elsewhere," not "user overrides don't matter."

### Gaps
- Print style sheets and HTML email: I found no sources this round; both are well-known cases where a document-level `@media print` or client-stripped CSS matters, but I have nothing citable. Treat as a gap.
- BetterDiscord/Discord theming: not fetched; I believe BetterDiscord themes are CSS files but could not verify, so do not assert it without a source.
- I found no source directly confirming how browser reader modes handle Shadow DOM content (whether text inside shadow roots is extracted). One search summary inferred that Readability-style extractors walking the light DOM would miss shadow content; unverified.
- No source quantifying how many users rely on user style sheets or forced-colors (Microsoft has historically cited ~4% of Windows users for high contrast, but I did not retrieve that this round).

## KQ3. Critiques of CSS-in-JS and sealed/scoped styles (runtime cost, token duplication, loss of inheritance, Shadow DOM's theming hole)

### Takeaway
Two independent lines of evidence: (1) runtime CSS-in-JS imposes measurable render cost and interacts badly with concurrent rendering (Magura, 2022, with Markbåge/Abramov in the thread); (2) Shadow DOM, the platform's own hard encapsulation, has spent 2020–2024 re-adding escape hatches — custom properties, `::part`/`exportparts`, open-stylable roots, `adoptStyles`/"shadow layers," and a still-unspecified `::theme()` — because sealed components cannot be themed, focus-styled or migrated to. The strongest single piece of evidence for the cascade side is Fagnani (Sept 2024): "Many people need to be able to arbitrarily style allowed elements deep down the shadow tree. That isn't solved by anything today."

### Cited Findings

**Sam Magura, "Why We're Breaking Up with CSS-in-JS" (DEV, 16 Oct 2022; updated 25 Oct 2022)** — written by an Emotion maintainer.
- The Good: "CSS-in-JS completely solves this problem by making styles locally-scoped by default" (but CSS Modules do too); colocation; JS variables in styles.
- The Bad: styles are serialised into CSS on each render; Emotion 7.9 kB / styled-components 12.7 kB minzipped vs 44.5 kB react+react-dom; DevTools clutter.
- Numbers (Member Browser, 20 users, M1 Max): Emotion with serialisation in render 54.3 ms avg over first 10 renders; Sass Modules 27.7 ms — "a 48% decrease." Rule of thumb ≤16 ms per render; each `<Box>` 0.1–0.2 ms; 54.3 ms "could reach 200 ms on a less powerful machine." Moving serialisation outside render helps but "prevents using props in styles."
- Concurrent rendering: quotes Sebastian Markbåge (React 18 WG): "This effectively causes a recalculation of all CSS rules against all DOM nodes every frame while React is rendering." Update 25 Oct 2022: applies to concurrent rendering without `useInsertionEffect`; Dan Abramov flagged the original as inaccurate; Magura still considers it unfixable within runtime CSS-in-JS. Also: SSR/component-library complexity, duplicate Emotion instances, style ordering issues. — [Magura](https://dev.to/srmagura/why-were-breaking-up-wiht-css-in-js-4g9b)

**Shadow DOM's theming hole and the escape hatches.**
- `::part()` "represents any element within a shadow tree that has a matching `part` attribute"; "The `::part()` pseudo-element is only visible to the parent DOM" (nested shadow trees need `exportparts`); structural pseudo-classes like `:empty`/`:last-child` "cannot be appended"; chained `::part(confirm-button)::part(active)` "never matches anything … because doing so would expose more structural information than is intended." — [MDN ::part](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/::part)
- WICG/webcomponents #909 "Open-stylable Shadow Roots," opened by Justin Fagnani 10 Dec 2020, still open: "strong style encapsulation is a major hinderance" to Shadow DOM adoption; drivers are legacy pages expecting global styles and tooling like Tailwind; proposed `attachShadow({mode: 'open-stylable'})`. Thread has grown to 250+ comments. — [WICG #909](https://github.com/WICG/webcomponents/issues/909)
- Fagnani, 4 Sept 2024 (CSSWG/WICG thread): "Many people need to be able to arbitrarily style allowed elements deep down the shadow tree. That isn't solved by anything today." … "Custom CSS properties have to be mapped by the component author, and `::part()` doesn't work deeply." … "`::theme()` was proposal long ago to solve this, but we haven't worked out the exact requirements." He separates open-stylable (migration from light DOM) from theming ("quite a different problem"). — [W3C list archive](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webapps-github/2024Sep/0058.html) (caveat: the first sentence appears with quote markers in the email and may be Fagnani quoting another participant)
- Earlier (Mar 2024) Fagnani said theming is better served by APIs that work with `::part()` without fully opening a shadow root; the search summary characterised him as calling open-stylable "a top blocker for web components adoption" but I could not verify that phrase in the fetched email. — [search summary citing lists.w3.org 2024Mar/0436](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webapps-github/2024Mar/0436.html)
- CSSWG issue #10176 (Apr 2024, Rob Glidden): `adoptStyles` capability for shadow roots, tied to #909, using declarative shadow DOM + cascade layers so a shadow tree can reference and prioritise document-level "page styles" ("Shadow Layers" proposal with polyfill). — [csswg-drafts #10176](https://github.com/w3c/csswg-drafts/issues/10176)
- Nolan Lawson, 30 Dec 2023: "When people complain about shadow DOM, they seem to mostly be complaining about style encapsulation." Component author must provide "an explicit styling API" via "CSS custom properties or parts." Consumers "can't just decide they want a background to be red." On open-stylable: "Will every web component on npm need to enable open-stylable shadow roots?" "What if a component author doesn't want to be opted-in?" — "a *coordination problem*, not a technical problem." Caridy Patiño's alternative ("Everything must be customizable from outside the shadow," Radix-style headless) shifts complexity to the consumer who may "copy-paste a lot of boilerplate" — a car sold as "a loose set of parts." "I'm also not convinced that any of this stuff is ripe enough for the standards discussion to begin." True component reusability is "still" a holy grail. — [Lawson](https://nolanlawson.com/2023/12/30/shadow-dom-and-the-problem-of-encapsulation/)
- Lawson's steelman *for* encapsulation (important for honesty): third-party dropdown restyled via global CSS; six months later a security update renames a class and adds a wrapper div — "Everything's broken." Component authors "effectively get frozen in time." He distinguishes throwaway sites from long-lived apps/design systems where maintainability dominates. — [Lawson](https://nolanlawson.com/2023/12/30/shadow-dom-and-the-problem-of-encapsulation/)
- Matuzo (23 Aug 2023), after a year of web components: "style encapsulation in web components isn't 100% encapsulation" (inheritable props leak in); "Style encapsulation sounds great until you want certain styles to apply everywhere" — global focus styles don't reach shadow DOM so he repeats them in every component with interactive elements; slotted overrides need `!important`; "You cannot style nested slotted content"; "If web components couldn't consume custom properties from the outside, I would've thrown them out of the window months ago"; many devs "default to light DOM and opt into shadow DOM only when it makes sense." — [Matuzo](https://matuzo.at/blog/2023/pros-and-cons-of-shadow-dom)
- Every Layout lists Shadow DOM as a drawback because "you can no longer leverage global styling." — [Every Layout](https://every-layout.dev/rudiments/global-and-local-styling/)

**"Loss of inheritance" / token duplication.**
- Lie's original rationale is the mirror image: cascading exists so "users and authors do not need to repeat all desired stylistic rules." — [Lie thesis](https://www.wiumlie.no/2006/phd/)
- Every Layout's axioms argument: applying a rule per element is "laborious, prone to error (missing elements out)." — [Every Layout](https://every-layout.dev/rudiments/axioms/)
- Matuzo's repeated-focus-styles complaint above is the concrete instance.

### Inferences
- The Shadow DOM story is the most persuasive evidence for the cascade side because it is not an opinion: the platform shipped hard encapsulation in 2016–2018, and the subsequent decade of standards work (parts 2019, open-stylable 2020→, adoptStyles 2024, `::theme()` still unspecified) is a sequence of attempts to punch cascade-shaped holes back in. The blog author should anticipate "your copy-and-configure templates are Shadow DOM without `::part`."
- But the same evidence supports a *token* thesis: every escape hatch that actually shipped (custom properties, `::part`) is a *named, author-declared contract*, not arbitrary selector reach. Fagnani explicitly says theming "is quite a different problem" from migration. The honest synthesis: hard encapsulation was wrong, *and* the fix converged on declared tokens plus declared parts — which is closer to the blog's position than to "global style sheets."
- Magura's numbers are from one app on one machine, with the maintainer of the library involved; they are credible but narrow. The concurrent-rendering point was partially retracted. Use with those caveats.

### Gaps
- Josh Comeau: no article found that treats global styles/inheritance trade-offs of CSS-in-JS; only a styled-components rebuild piece and podcasts. Kent C. Dodds: not searched (budget). Tailwind critiques: not researched beyond Keith's "imperative" framing and Fagnani's note that Tailwind breaks under Shadow DOM.
- No source naming the "every component sets its own font" anti-pattern in those words. Matuzo's repeated focus-style and Every Layout's "missing elements out" are the closest citable analogues.
- Could not retrieve a measured token-duplication study for CSS-in-JS codebases.

## KQ4. Do `@layer`, `@scope`, container queries, custom properties and `:has` make the cascade component-friendly rather than obsolete? (Suzanne, Bramus, Kravets)

### Takeaway
The spec authors' stated position is that scoping and layering are *additions to the cascade*, deliberately weaker than specificity and explicitly not isolation: `@scope` "limits the reach of the selectors. It doesn't offer style isolation" (Bramus) and "Cascade layers do not solve scoping" (Kravets). Suzanne's explainer says the proposal "intentionally" made proximity weaker than specificity so global styles can "flow through, interact with, and even override scoped styles." In other words, the cascade's custodians looked at component encapsulation and built the opposite.

### Cited Findings

**`@scope` (Suzanne's explainer; Bramus, Chrome, 4 Oct 2023)**
- Problem framing: "All CSS Selectors are global, matching against the entire DOM"; BEM naming is a workaround; nested themes expose that selectors "are not weighted by 'proximity' to the element being styled," so source order decides and the outer theme wins; components have "Occasional 'donut holes' or 'slots' where sub-components can be nested." — [Scope explainer](https://css.oddbird.net/scope/explainer/)
- Two approaches distinguished: "Total isolation of a component DOM subtree/fragment from the host page" vs "Lighter-touch, style-driven namespacing." Isolation is a non-goal: "We have not attempted to address that form of scope in this proposal"; it "feels like a significantly different approach that already has work underway." "Shadow-DOM is not designed for the common 'design system' approach." Proximity is "weak proximity" by design: "We have intentionally gone in the other direction, making scope proximity less powerful than specificity in the cascade." Encapsulation is left to Shadow DOM ("encapsulation context can be expanded and improved on for the high-isolation use-cases"). — [Scope explainer](https://css.oddbird.net/scope/explainer/)
- Bramus: `@scope` lets you write selectors that are specific yet "easy to override and not be tightly coupled to the DOM structure"; the prelude selectors "do not affect the specificity of the contained selectors"; adds "a new criterion: scoping proximity," after specificity and before order of appearance; "the declaration with the fewest generational or sibling-element hops" wins; "Know that `@scope` limits the reach of the selectors. It doesn't offer style isolation" — inherited properties such as `color` still pass through the donut's hole; upper+lower boundary is "often referred to as a donut scope." Alternatives named: Vue Scoped CSS, Styled Components, BEM. — [Bramus, Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/css-ui/at-scope)

**`@layer` (Suzanne's explainer; Una Kravets, Chrome, Feb 2022)**
- Problem: within one origin "the primary tools available within a single origin are specificity and source order"; managing it needs "careful management of selector-specificity, or over-use of `!important` flags"; "third-party tools can easily break that delicate balance"; BEM/ITCSS try to keep specificity flat or matched to layers. Goal: "deterministic style ordering" letting authors layer tokens/defaults/patterns/components and "define their own layering scheme and avoid specificity or source-order conflicts across concerns." Frames origins as the model: "Cascade Origins help to balance styling concerns across stakeholders … It's a pretty clever solution, with `!important` providing much-needed counter-balance." — [Layers explainer](https://css.oddbird.net/layers/explainer/)
- Kravets: `@layer` gives "more explicit control of your CSS files to prevent style-specificity conflicts"; "the precedence of a layer always beats the specificity of a selector"; "particularly useful for large codebases, design systems, and when managing third party styles in applications"; "Using a reset.css file … is a good starting point and use case"; component styles can live in a `components` layer with utilities layered above; but "Cascade layers do not solve scoping" (you still write `.card a`); layered styles don't override unlayered CSS; `!important` reverses layer order. — [Kravets](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/cascade-layers)
- Eckles (Smashing, 24 Jan 2022): layers "are not meant to solve scoping or encapsulation"; native scoping is a separate spec Suzanne is also writing; layers "return control of specificity and source order to authors." — [Eckles](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2022/01/introduction-css-cascade-layers/)
- Bramus via Stoumann (10 Jan 2022): "The true power of Cascade Layers comes from its unique position in the Cascade"; `:where` yields "literally zero" specificity so component defaults can be overridden by anything later. — [Stoumann](https://css-tricks.com/dont-fight-the-cascade-control-it/)

**Custom properties through shadow boundaries / cascade layers into shadow roots**
- CSSWG #10176 proposes `adoptStyles` so a shadow tree can reference and prioritise document-level "page styles" *via cascade layers* — i.e. the proposed bridge between encapsulation and global styles is itself the layering primitive. — [csswg-drafts #10176](https://github.com/w3c/csswg-drafts/issues/10176)
- Matuzo and Lawson (KQ3) both identify custom properties as the one mechanism that reliably crosses the shadow boundary.

### Inferences
- The "component-friendly cascade" claim is best stated as: `@layer` fixes the *ordering* problem that BEM/CSS-in-JS were partly invented to avoid (specificity wars, third-party overrides), `@scope` fixes *leakage* with donut holes for nested components, and both leave inheritance and global override intact on purpose. The blog's counter should engage with the deliberate "weak proximity" choice: the spec authors considered isolation and rejected it for the design-system case.
- Container queries and `:has()` were not researched this round (budget); Suzanne's "styling unknown content … on an infinite and unknowable canvas" framing is the rhetorical home for container queries, but I have no direct quote tying CQ to the cascade debate.

### Gaps
- No primary Una Kravets or Bramus statement about `@scope` *versus* Shadow DOM; Bramus's article explicitly does not compare them.
- No Suzanne quote on container queries or `:has` in relation to components.
- Lea Verou's archived notes on the scope proposal (css.oddbird.net syntax page) surfaced but were not fetched.

## KQ5. The "component templates" critique from the other direction: do copy-and-configure templates drift? (design-system literature on snowflakes, drift, central enforcement)

### Takeaway
The design-system literature treats drift as the central governance problem and agrees that one-off "snowflake" components are a source of it, but the practitioner consensus (Frost/Rivera, Prototypr) is to *govern* snowflakes, not forbid them — and the proposed remedies are tokens, lifecycle ownership and audits, not a global style sheet. I found no empirical study showing that copy-and-configure templates drift more than cascade-based systems; the evidence is practitioner and vendor opinion.

### Cited Findings
- Brad Frost (3 Feb 2021), summarising Mike Rivera: design system components are "content-agnostic, and context-agnostic built for maximal reuse"; recipes are compositions "used consistently across one product"; snowflakes are one-offs that "don't really get reused outside of their first use case" (e.g. airline Seat). "Polluting a design system library with a bunch of product-specific components gets noisy and messy." Does **not** discuss drift, tokens or global styles. — [Frost](https://bradfrost.com/blog/post/design-system-components-recipes-and-snowflakes/)
- Prototypr: the drive for consistency can crowd out "the flexibility needed to craft the right element for the right situation"; recommends documenting purpose, use cases and why existing components won't work, then proceeding. — [Prototypr](https://blog.prototypr.io/why-snowflakes-are-counterintuitively-integral-to-design-systems-654dac639d81)
- Always Twisted: generate utility classes from design tokens so teams "can build custom components that still align with the overall visual language of the Design System." — [Always Twisted](https://www.alwaystwisted.com/articles/creating-design-system-friendly-snowflakes-with-utility-classes)
- Vendor definition of drift: "the gradual mismatch between intended UI patterns and what shows up in production … the design system might be perfectly intact, but what's shipped doesn't match it." — [Webstacks](https://www.webstacks.com/blog/ai-design-system-monitoring)
- Drift mechanism: "accumulated small modifications—4px padding adjustments, slightly darker color values, modified interaction states—that individually seem harmless but collectively fragment visual coherence." — [21st.dev audit guide](https://21st.dev/blog/design-system-consistency-audit)
- Governance over tooling: "a lightweight component lifecycle with clear owners and decision rights prevents more drift than any review checklist"; measure reuse rates, design-code alignment, accessibility and adoption quarterly. — [Magic Patterns (16 May 2026)](https://www.magicpatterns.com/blog/design-system-maintenance)
- Figma-AI anecdote: Figma AI "happily picks up your snowflakes instead of your proper design system ones because, well, it can't tell them apart." — [Design Systems Collective (member-only)](https://www.designsystemscollective.com/when-figma-ai-met-my-snowflake-components-it-got-ugly-7f7cfe4d82a9)
- Cascade-side analogue: Every Layout's claim that hand-applying rules per element is "prone to error (missing elements out)" and that a `:root` custom property means "any change to the value will be propagated throughout the design" is the pro-cascade mechanism for *preventing* the 4px-padding drift described above. — [Every Layout](https://every-layout.dev/rudiments/axioms/)
- Lawson's reverse case: with global CSS the *consumer's* overrides drift out of sync with the component ("Everything's broken" after an internal refactor). — [Lawson](https://nolanlawson.com/2023/12/30/shadow-dom-and-the-problem-of-encapsulation/)

### Inferences
- The drift literature is agnostic between "global style layer" and "token layer"; both are "central enforcement." The only thing it clearly argues against is *ungoverned copying*, which is the Roblox-style copy-and-configure template's failure mode if there is no upstream link. The blog author can claim tokens as the enforcement mechanism, but must then answer Fagnani's point that tokens only cover what the author pre-declared.
- Webstacks, 21st.dev and Magic Patterns are vendor content; Frost/Rivera is the only widely-cited practitioner framework in the set.

### Gaps
- No empirical study of drift rates in copy-and-configure vs. linked-instance vs. cascade systems was found. Nathan Curtis (EightShapes) and Figma's own "detach instance" literature were not searched.
- No evidence specific to Roblox UI templates.

## KQ6. Academic / structured analyses of CSS maintainability and duplication

### Takeaway
The peer-reviewed record says real-world CSS is heavily duplicated (median 68% of declarations involved in at least one clone across 38 sites/91 files, vs. 5–20% in procedural/OO code) and heavily unused (average 60% of selectors across 15 apps), and the papers explicitly blame "inheritance, cascading and specificity" for making CSS hard to maintain. This is double-edged: it is evidence that the cascade *as practised* fails, but the duplication findings are also exactly what the cascade's defenders say happens when you stop using inheritance and start repeating declarations per selector. No study was found that measures whether component-scoped styling increases or decreases duplication.

### Cited Findings
- Mazinanian, Tsantalis & Mesbah, "Discovering Refactoring Opportunities in Cascading Style Sheets," FSE 2014, pp. 496–506. Abstract: CSS "exhibits complex features, such as inheritance, cascading and specificity, which make CSS code hard to maintain." Evaluated on "38 real-world web systems and 91 CSS files"; "duplication in CSS code is widely prevalent." Results: duplication "is indeed very intense ranging from 40% to 90%"; "The median value for the percentage of duplicated declarations is 68%, while the average is 66%," vs "5–20% duplicated code" in procedural/OO code; 97% of clones are type I (exact) duplication; presentation-preserving refactoring gave "average size reduction … 8%, while the maximum achieved value was 35%"; in 12% of files (11/91) reduction exceeded a threshold. Also notes CSS "consumes 40–70% of the average processing time of the browser" (citing prior work) and examples from Gmail's inbox CSS. — [FSE 2014 PDF](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/amesbah/resources/papers/fse14.pdf)
- Companion tool paper: Mazinanian & Tsantalis, "CSSDev: refactoring duplication in cascading style sheets," ICSE Companion 2017, pp. 63–66. — [DBLP](https://dblp.org/pid/87/4206)
- Mesbah & Mirshokraie, "Automated Analysis of CSS Rules to Support Style Maintenance," ICSE 2012 (CILLA tool). Abstract: CSS "has a number of characteristics, such as inheritance and cascading order, which makes maintaining CSS code a challenging task … it is common for unused rules to be accumulated over time"; case study "on fifteen open source and industrial web-based systems, show an average of 60% unused CSS selectors in deployed applications." — [ICSE 2012 PDF](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/amesbah/resources/papers/icse12.pdf); [CILLA tool](https://github.com/saltlab/cilla/)
- Gharachorlu, UBC MSc thesis 2014, "Code smells in cascading style sheets": corpus of 500 websites, 5,060 CSS files, >10 million lines; separate unused-CSS analysis on 187 sites (specific percentage not retrieved). — [UBC library record](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0167067)
- Keller & Nussbaumer, "CSS Code Quality: A Metric for Abstractness – Or Why Humans Beat Machines in CSS Coding," QUATIC 2010, pp. 116–121, DOI 10.1109/QUATIC.2010.25. A later review describes it as proposing a quality metric "based on an abstractness factor to explain why human-written CSS was better than code generated automatically by tools," intended as a benchmark for authoring tools. Their WWW 2009 poster (reverse-generating CSS from desired visual effects, tested on stylesheets from 25,000 HTML documents) reported machine-generated CSS "is close to hand-written CSS" on their criteria. — [KIT record](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000020089); [WWW 2009 poster PDF](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2009/proceedings/pdf/p1161.pdf)
  - Inference (mine, from title + review): "abstractness" rewards fewer, more general rules, i.e. exploiting inheritance/selector reach rather than enumerating per-element declarations — which would make it a formalisation of the Every Layout "specificity ∝ 1/reach" principle. Unverified; full text not obtained.
- On the fragility of "unused CSS" numbers: one CSS-Tricks measurement found ~55% unused after load but ~15% after interacting with all tabs; any single figure "depends on the interaction state." — [CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/?p=264937)
- Current field data: Core Web Vitals explorer (Q2 2026, 189,915 desktop sites) reports ~307 KB of CSS on the typical page never used during load (byte-level, load-time only; not comparable to selector-level studies). — [corewebvitals.io](https://www.corewebvitals.io/state-of-cwv/explorer/metrics.stylesheets.coverage/desktop)

### Inferences
- For the blog author: the academic literature is *not* a steelman for the cascade. Every paper's motivation section names inheritance/cascading/specificity as the maintainability hazard. The cascade defender's reply has to be "these are studies of CSS written without discipline; a `:root`-axioms + `@layer` codebase would not show 68% duplication" — and there is no study testing that.
- Equally, there is no study showing component-scoped styling reduces duplication; the FSE14 duplication types (same declarations repeated across selectors) are precisely what CSS Modules / CSS-in-JS per-component files would produce *more* of unless tokens are shared.

### Gaps
- Mazinanian & Tsantalis's preprocessor studies (SANER 2016 "An Empirical Study on the Use of CSS Preprocessors"; ASE 2016 "Migrating CSS to Preprocessors by Introducing Mixins") were not retrieved; they would show whether mixins/variables (the token layer) actually reduce duplication in practice.
- Keller & Nussbaumer full text and the abstractness formula were not obtained (IEEE paywall).
- No academic study of CSS-in-JS, CSS Modules or Shadow DOM maintainability was found.
- "Tool Support for Cascading Style Sheets Complexity Metrics" (2015) and "A Complexity Metrics Suite for Cascading Style Sheets" surfaced in search but were not read.
